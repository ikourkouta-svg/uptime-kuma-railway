# Email to Costa — MVP / Lean Startup argument

**Subject:** Why MRX doesn't need big teams or big budgets to launch new things

---

Dear Costa,

Following our directors' meetings these past weeks, I want to put my argument in writing. In the room it comes out as a disagreement about one initiative or another, and that is not what it is. The disagreement is about method — about what it actually costs to find out whether a new idea is worth anything — and I don't think I have made the case properly out loud.

Let me state your position as fairly as I can, because I don't think it's unreasonable: a serious launch deserves serious resources. A proper team, a proper budget, a proper product, a proper go-to-market. Anything less looks amateurish, risks the MRX name, and wastes everyone's time on half-built things. If we are going to do something, we should do it well.

I agree with the last sentence completely. I disagree that "doing it well" and "spending big up front" are the same thing. In my experience they are frequently opposites, and I'd like to explain why.

## The core mistake: treating a new idea like a known business

When we open a new site, add a line to an existing operation, or expand something that already works, we know what we are doing. The uncertainty is in execution — cost, timing, staffing. Big teams and real budgets are exactly right there, because the question is *can we deliver this well*, and money buys delivery.

A genuinely new product, service or company is a different animal. The uncertainty is not in execution. It is in the assumptions: that a specific customer has this problem, badly enough to pay, and will choose us over what they do today. Money does not buy the answer to that. It only buys a bigger, more expensive, more public version of whatever answer was already there.

This is why the failures hurt so much more than they should. A large team with a large budget doesn't fail cheaply — it fails eighteen months in, with a finished product, contracts signed, people hired, and a reputation attached. And because so much has been committed, nobody can afford to say it isn't working. That is the real risk to the MRX name: not launching something small, but being unable to stop something large.

## What an MVP actually is — and what it is not

The term has been ruined by people using it as an excuse, so let me be precise.

A Minimum Viable Product is **not** a cheap product. It is not a stripped-down version we're embarrassed by. It is not permission to ship something sloppy under the MRX name.

An MVP is **the smallest thing we can build that produces a real answer to the riskiest assumption in the plan.** Its output is not revenue. Its output is knowledge — what Eric Ries calls *validated learning*: a specific belief, tested against real customer behaviour, confirmed or killed.

The discipline is in the word "riskiest." For any new MRX initiative, we should be able to write on one page: *this only works if X is true.* Then we build the cheapest honest test of X and nothing else.

Very often that test isn't software or a factory at all. It's a landing page and a price. It's selling the service manually to ten customers before we automate anything. It's one salesperson with a presentation and a signed letter of intent. These sound unserious. They are not — they are the same questions the eighteen-month version answers, asked for a fraction of the money and time.

The loop is simple, and it is the whole method: **Build → Measure → Learn**, then decide to persevere or pivot. A pivot is not a failure; it is a change of course that keeps everything we learned. Failure is completing the loop and ignoring the result.

## Why volume matters — and why speed is the real budget

Here is the part I most want you to accept, because everything else follows from it.

Returns on new ventures do not distribute evenly. They follow a power law: out of ten serious attempts, most return nothing, a couple return something modest, and one — if we're good and lucky — returns more than all the others combined. This is not pessimism about our judgement. It is true of every venture portfolio ever measured, run by people who do nothing else for a living.

Two conclusions follow, and they are both uncomfortable:

**We cannot pick the winner in advance.** Not you, not me, not the board. If we could, the whole industry would look different. Our conviction in a meeting is not evidence.

**Therefore the number of attempts matters as much as the quality of each one.** If we can only afford two attempts per year because each one costs a full team and a full budget, we have bought two lottery tickets and bet the group's future on being right about which. If the same money funds ten small, honest tests, we have ten chances to find the one that works — and, critically, we have the money left to *pour everything into it* when we find it.

That last point is the one people miss. Running many small experiments is not spreading ourselves thin. It is the opposite: it is how we earn the right to concentrate. We stay cheap and plural while we are *searching*, so that we can be expensive and singular once we've *found*. The big team and the big budget you want are not cancelled — they are earned, deployed behind evidence instead of opinion.

Which brings me to speed. Time is our scarcest input, not cash. Every month an initiative runs unvalidated is a month of salaries, attention and management focus we cannot recover. The measure of a good innovation process is not how much it spends but **how many complete learning cycles it can run per year.** Fast is not a style preference. Fast is the budget.

## Fast, but not sloppy — where the line sits

You will rightly worry that "fast and many" becomes "careless and embarrassing." So let me be exact about where quality is non-negotiable and where it is not.

**Never compromise:** anything the customer actually touches — accuracy, safety, legal and regulatory compliance, data protection, and how we treat the people who trust us with a first purchase. Nor honesty: if something is a pilot, we say so.

**Deliberately compromise:** scale, automation, polish, feature breadth, internal tooling, and anything built for a volume of customers we do not yet have. Manual is fine. Ugly is fine. Narrow is fine. Ten customers served brilliantly by hand teaches us more than ten thousand served by a system nobody wanted.

Sloppy means the customer gets a bad experience. Lean means *we* do unglamorous work so the customer doesn't have to. They are not the same thing, and I will hold any team of mine to that distinction.

## The other half: be small in resources, not in ambition

I don't want to leave you with the impression that I'm arguing for timidity, because I'm not — and this is where Peter Thiel's *Zero to One* corrects the lean method rather than agreeing with it.

Thiel's warning is that endless iteration produces endless small improvements — going from 1 to *n*, copying what exists, competing on margin. The prizes go to whoever creates something genuinely new — 0 to 1 — and the test is whether we are **ten times better** at something, not ten percent. Ten percent gets competed away. Ten times gets a market.

His practical advice is the one I'd most like us to adopt: **start by dominating a small market completely.** Not one percent of a huge market — that is a fantasy that has justified more bad plans than any other sentence in business. A specific, small, winnable segment where MRX can be the obvious best choice, and from which we can expand outward once we own it.

So the synthesis I'm proposing is not "think small." It is:

> **Bold in ambition, cheap in validation.** Aim at something that could genuinely matter to the group in ten years. Find out whether it's real with the smallest, fastest, most honest test we can design — and be willing to hear no.

## What I'm asking for concretely

Not a change of philosophy in the abstract — a change in how we approve things. My proposal for the next directors' meeting:

1. **Separate the two decisions.** Funding a *search* (is there a business here?) and funding a *build* (let's construct it properly) are different decisions with different sizes, different committees and different success criteria. Today we treat every new idea as a build, which is why every new idea is expensive and slow.
2. **A fixed, small envelope for search.** A defined budget and a hard time limit per idea — enough for a small team to run a real test, not enough to build a finished product. Small teams are a feature: two or three people who own the whole problem move faster and learn more than fifteen who each own a slice.
3. **Written assumptions and kill criteria, agreed before we start.** For each initiative: what must be true, what we will measure, and what result means we stop. Agreed *in advance*, so that stopping is a success of the process rather than someone's personal defeat. This is the single change that would save MRX the most money.
4. **A regular review of the whole portfolio, not one idea at a time.** Judged on learning per euro and per month, not on how impressive the deck looks. Some will be killed at every review. That is the system working, not failing.
5. **Real concentration behind whatever survives.** When something clears the evidence bar, it gets the full weight of the group — team, budget, distribution, the MRX name. That's the trade I'm offering: discipline and modesty at the start, in exchange for genuine commitment when it's warranted.

Costa, I'm not asking you to take any of this on faith. I'm asking for one initiative — you choose which — to be run this way for a fixed period, against criteria we agree together in advance. If the method is wrong, we will have found that out cheaply, which is rather the point.

I'd welcome the chance to walk through this before the next meeting, at whatever length you want to give it.
